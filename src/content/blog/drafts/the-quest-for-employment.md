I finally found a job. Fortunately, it's not just any job. It's a job that actually excites me. More about that later.

This marks the first time I've had to look for a job since I was out of college. My first job spanned the course of 5 years. My next job was running a startup, spun out of internal tech from my first job, for the course of 5 years. While winding down my time at the startup, I decided to take 3 months to relax and figure out what I wanted next for myself before starting the actual job hunt. During this time I:
- Built out my personal website
- Trained and competed in the World Series of Board Games
- Went to see the Rocket League World Series in person
- Traveled to Japan and South Korea
- Ramped up seeing live music
- Created a TikTok to make songs on random topics 
- Started Improv Classes

The time off was exhilirating. For the first time in a long time, I had the full ability to do whatever I wanted, whenever I wanted. I learned to indulge my curiosity and see where it took me. 

However, once I got back from traveling, I had to get myself back into the mindset of looking for work. I heard from multiple sources online that the current job market typically took folks 7-9 months in order to find a new role. Thankfully, I was mentally and financially prepared for this timeline.

The first step was figuring out what roles I wanted to apply for. Initially, I started out by looking for Head/Director of Data roles (title depending on company size) and Chief of Staff roles. While I think I'd be well-suited for the chief of staff role, I found that very few were in the Austin area or remote. I also realized that a role at that level might be too high-stress while I was making efforts to reset my relationship with work. As I started digging more into the types of roles that were available, I started realizing that Product Manager roles for Data Platforms and Technical Product Managers were both up my alley.

Where I landed was:
- Data (Department Lead), AI/Innovation (Department Lead), or Product (Senior/Staff) role
- Smaller company preferred, but larger companies not off the table
- Happy initially being IC if there's growth potential

Yes, it's a wide array of jobs. But frankly, over my time working, I've picked up a wide array of skills and interests. I really had no desire  to pigeonhole myself.

As I continued the application process, I evaluated what was working and what wasn't, making changes to hopefully improve my odds of getting through.
- In late November, I reduced my resume down to one page and consolidated my role bullets. I figured that if two roles were just promotion extensions of each other, with roughly the same responsibilities, it wasn't worth detailing both.
- In mid-December, I got a bit more targeted with my bullet keywords. I made bullets related to "Data Innovation" instead say "Data Science". I made more references to analytics and PLG metrics.
- In late December, I rebuilt my cover letter with AI to have three templated variations for Data, Product, and AI applications. I left a section at the top to customize it for each role and company, plus a sentence or two for excitement about the specific problem space. Frankly, I was surprised that the AI editing did a much better job at invoking the tone of voice I wanted than my original writing did. [^1]


Observations:
- Way too many applications result in you getting ghosted. For someone with 10+ years of leadership experience running businesses and building teams from the ground up, I thought my success rate of getting to the screener would be higher.
- I estimate that half of the rejections were just fully automated "we filled this role" rejections vs "we looked at your application and felt you weren't a good fit" rejections. I base this assumption on the time it took to receive a rejection from the initial application. 
- Getting to the screener phase was extremely difficult. My general sentiment was that if I got on a call, I could easily sell myself. My anecdata tells me that it was easier to get a screener if the company had established presence in Austin OR if they were small enough to get the attention of someone higher up.
  - For one startup, I reached out directly to the CEO to share my thoughts on product opportunities. That resulted in a pretty instant screener with them.
  - For another startup, I shared my project digging into their product on social media and got retweeted by the CTO. I'm convinced that accelerated my chances.
  - For another startup, I connected with the executive team on twitter and engaged on their posts.
- Of the 7 interview cycles I entered, only one rejection was really a surprise. In most cases, having a call made it easier to identify when it wasn't a good fit and those reasons were almost never something that could be gleaned from the application. Unstated rejection reasons were things like differing product vision, lack of internal AI prioritization. Stated rejection reasons were not enough experience with specific industries (government, healthcare).
- From most interview processes, I learned that most companies don't know _exactly_ what they're looking for in a new hire. The stated desires or the questions asked often times didn't match the job description. For example [^2]:
  - A Head of AI role stated they wanted someone to direct the AI strategy and innovation, manage a team of engineers and data scientists, stay on top of trends, and represent the company at conferences and in media. Given my past implementing AI at organizations, running technical data teams, and representing a brand through podcasts and conferences, I thought it was a great fit. In the interview process, I was grilled on various models and knowledge of terminology/implementation, ultimately rejected for not being technical enough. Clear mismatch of expectations.
  - A Head of Business Insights role where they were very specific about checking off boxes for using buzzwords like "Kimball", "Star Schema", or "Medallion Architecture" that were more Data Engineering geared. When I mentioned having direct exposure to cloud databases like BigQuery, Snowflake, and Redshift there was concern that I didn't mention Databricks.
  - A Director of Product Analytics role whose job description focused on integrating 10+ platforms internally and being the point of contact for new integrations and datasets. When interviewed, I was mostly asked questions about telling stories with data. Afterwards, I was told that I was a better fit for the Director of Data Engineering role (read: we think you're too technical) but given my lack of pure infrastructure knowledge, I didn't think that was the best fit. 
- There were a number of people that posted on LinkedIn saying "Hey, I'm hiring for this role! If you're interested, please reach out to me directly." Every single one of those that I reached out to ghosted me.
- The longer time went on, the better idea I had about what companies and roles interested me. There were a lot that I was just "meh" on. Finding the perfect fit and getting the timing right (being on the market exactly when they're looking) is a hard thing to pull off.
- HackerNews "Who Wants to Be Hired" was a total dud:
  - I was only contacted for two legitimate opportunities. In one instance, they clearly hadn't read my post. In the other instance, it wasn't clear if they were looking to hire, chat, or just wanted consulting work done. 
  - My email got spammed with irrelevant messages. Good thing I used a masked email that I can shut down now.
- From the job descriptions I read, companies seem to be preferring individuals that have a deep amount of experience in one area of work within a single industry. To me, this seems short-sighted. I think a wide exposure to different roles and different industries brings more value due to diversity of thought and experience. However, many seem like they would prefer 8+ years of ONLY data engineering work ONLY working on B2C applications.
- There's still too much of an emphasis on having a specific college degree. 
  - I don't think I saw a single job that didn't list at least a bachelors as a requirement, with many preferring a masters. 
  - All of them specify a field of study. It's humorous to me that the jobs I would be best at (data) look for computer science and math, something I'll never qualify for. 


## My Feelings
Historically, when I've met people looking for work, I categorized it as a very objective thing. "Oh, they just don't have a job right now." I never factored in everything that went into that statement.

Realistically, every day of looking for a job is an emotional roller coaster. 
- You're exhausted from searching through job applications, repeating the same fields, with no end in sight and no real feedback.
- You're filled with excitement when you find a role that seems like a perfect match with a company you're really excited by.
- You're devastated when you get rejected from those jobs that seem like a perfect match.
- You're full of hope when you finally get a screener with a company.
- You're in full-on damage control mode when people ask you how the job search is going. How honest should you be? What do people think about how long it's taking you? 

I think the biggest issue for me personally was feeling inadequate, like there was always _something_ about me that someone would pick up on and think made me not the right fit. Due to the constant rejection (or lack of response) I had to make up stories for why I wasn't getting any further in the interview process. Thoughts that entered my head:
- As an ex-founder, I was a risk for being hard to manage. Some hiring managers may not want someone reporting to them that they think is going to constantly push and challenge them. [^3]
- If I applied for a less senior title, I would be seen as a flight risk for going "below my station". If I applied for a more senior title, I would be seen as overconfident and going "above my station". [^4] 
- With all of my recent titled experience being leadership, there were concerns about my ability to be a hands-on-keyboard individual contributor, despite the fact that early-stage founder work skews heavily 
- No one was quite sure what I was best at. Was I more of a data engineer? Data scientist? Product Manager? 
- I was in a purgatory of being both too technical and not technical enough. It was a toss-up how people felt about my resume and the conversations with me.

All of these statements were probably true and not true at the same time. In the moment, when you really want a job, it's hard to see that you don't want to work at places where you're not the right fit. You want a place that wants you for you. [^5]

I'm grateful to have experienced unemployment so that I can have more empathy and insight into what it looks like.

## My Frustrations
- At the beginning of an application, there's no easy way to let a company know that you're HIGHLY interested in them. You can try to imbue as much enthusiasm as possible into the cover letter (if it's an option) but most hiring managers just glaze over these. I want a "Fave 5" for companies that I can't game.
- The ghosting is ridiculous at this point. Recruiters and hiring managers should be held accountable for giving everyone that applies a response within a specific time frame, otherwise the application "auto-expires".
- Tracking job applications is a needlessly annoying task. Because an employer can take a job down at any moment, it's up to you track every job you apply to, save the job description as a PDF, and verify status (applied, interview, rejected). If you don't do this, you're at a risk of no longer having the job description be available if you get contacted for an interview Now that I've deployed my website to CloudFlare, I realized that I need to update the Supabase authentication. Currently, I was hardcoding localhost colon 3000, but now I need to update it to the actual URL, which is ghostlessjobs.com. However, I want to make sure that I can still test this locally. How do I make sure that when I run the code, run it locally? It uses local host and when I run it live on ghostlessjobs.com it uses that URL. 
- Custom application questions are mixed bag. On one hand, I **hate** the extra steps required just to apply. On the other hand I **love** that they made me think more critically and do more involved research on the company. Many of the questions made me dig deeper into my past and surfaced experience that could easily be turned into blog posts. These applications were more annoying when when you got auto-rejected after putting 1-2 hours into the responses, but I think that they also result in a higher signal-to-noise ratio for the employer.

## The End of the Quest

At the end of February, I started a new role with Roboflow as their first analytics hire. They are a computer vision platform that helps you build and deploy systems that turn the real-world into data. While it's an oversimplification, I like to think they convert IRL to JSON.

That means I'll be building a lot of the initial data pipelines and figuring out how to use data to scale the business. I'm super excited by the opportunity as there's a lot of long-term potential in the computer 

They checked all the boxes for me:
- Tackling an audacious problem that's tangential to AI applications
- Distributed Workforce (Remote, but lots of in-person opportunities)
- Small enough to make a high impact (~65 employees)
- First or early data hire, giving more ability to establish a norm and grow
- High Autonomy to solve problems (1/3 of hires are previous founders)

I remember telling my wife and parents about the role after the initial conversation with the hiring manager. The opportunity "felt right" but I told them that I couldn't get emotionally attached to it because otherwise I had the potential to be devastated by a rejection.

I'm kinda surprised I found the role at the right moment too. I was scouring YCombinator's "Work at a Startup" job board, looking through every job sorted by "Newest". I had to browse this way because titles at early stage companies are often imperfect.
