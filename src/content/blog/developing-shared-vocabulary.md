## Shared Vocabulary
In a lot of ways, shared vocabulary can be a great thing. It's a way to distill complex ideas into just a few words. In my own hobbies of board games and video games, they become easy paths to understanding the core mechanics of the game and either getting up to speed (to play) or for determining enjoyment (to buy).

### Board Games
**Rondel** - Actions that you take will be spread around a wheel. Either the wheel itself will rotate or your movement of objects around the wheel will continue to rotate, making it unlikely that the same action can be taken twice. 

**Deck Builder** - Over the course of the game, you will be gaining more cards and adding them to your deck. Every card that you buy reduces the probability that it will be playable in a future turn.

### Video Games
**Platformer** - The core game mechanic revolves around movement, using your skills (typically jumping) to get from platform to platform.

### AI
**Agentic Workflows** - The system prompts an AI model in a way where the exact instructions are not provided. Instead, the AI model must take the problem and "reason" about the most effective steps to reach a solution. Each step is then provided back to the AI model to work on until either the steps are finished or the problem is solved.

**Prompt Engineering** - Finding the best possible set of written instructions to get an AI model to return the output that most closely matches what you envisioned.

## The Downsides

The terms themselves are not important. The concepts that they represent are. The main problem with shared vocabulary is the individual baggage that one brings along with the definition from their own experience.

- If your only video game experience is with a classic Mario title, you may think that all platformers need to be in 2D, scrolling left to right.
- If your only board game experience is with Catan, you may think that a deck builder will always involve spending your cards.
- If your only AI experience is with ChatGPT, you may think that prompt engineering is just specifying the role that you want a chatbot to play when responding to you.

This negative baggage can make it incredibly difficult for someone else to understand what you're talking about.
I faced this sort of negative baggage all the time while building out my startup. We stated that we were building a "data orchestration" tool, but to many, the term orchestration meant "system that schedules and kicks off other services", not "system that processes and moves data".
